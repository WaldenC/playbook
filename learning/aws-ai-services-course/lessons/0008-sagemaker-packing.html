Lesson 0008 · Packing

One GPU, many tenants

A GPU that's 20% busy still bills 100%. Inference components are how SageMaker slices one instance across several models — and the platform sells you the utilization back.

The idle GPU is the expensive GPU

A dedicated endpoint per model is the naive default, and it bleeds money. Inference traffic is bursty — the lunch hour spikes, the midnight quiet — so a whole instance reserved for one small model sits mostly empty, billing the full rate either way.

The fix is packing: slice the instance, not the bill. An inference component is one model's slice of a GPU instance — its own container, its own GPU allotment, its own autoscaling policy.

One g5.12xlarge, two tenants

Here's the canonical shape: on a single g5.12xlarge, Stable Diffusion gets 1 GPU and Llama 2 gets 2. Each component autoscales independently — Llama can spin up copies while Stable Diffusion sleeps, and each only pays for its own slot.

Model pinning keeps N warm copies of a component standing by, so a surge never falls into the cold-start trap from lesson 0006. Least-connections routing hands each request to the warm copy with the fewest outstanding calls — load spreads by current pressure, not round-robin luck.

The KV-cache bonus

The next trick sits inside the serving engine, and it landed on HyperPod in November 2025. A key-value (KV) cache stores the conversation's earlier tokens so they aren't recomputed; a tiered KV cache spreads those tensors across fast and slow tiers, and intelligent routing steers each request to a copy whose cache already covers most of its context.

The reported numbers: up to 40% latency reduction and a 25% throughput gain; for Llama-3.1-70B, the median (P50) time to first token (TTFT) drops 72% at 8k context and 94% at 64k. Longer contexts mean heavier caches, so the win grows with them.

Repacking without breaking traffic

Changing a component — a new model version, a new GPU split — is a deployment, not a restart. SageMaker rolls the update across copies with four strategies: all-at-once, canary, linear, or blue-green, and you can pin capacity percentages per component while it updates.

Canary shifts a few percent of traffic first and watches; blue-green builds a fully warmed replacement and flips, rolling back if it misbehaves. The pinned warm copies are what make those flips cheap.

Rolling-update strategies and per-component capacity percentages: the same post's update guidance.

The whole game is utilization. The same g5.12xlarge can sit at 20% or 80% busy — the GPU bills identically either way. Packing is that difference, and what SageMaker sells you is exactly it: a slot that always has a tenant.

Utilization or latency?

Two small models idle on separate endpoints. What raises utilization?

Packing is the utilization lever — one instance, many components, each autoscaling on its own traffic. Fast Model Loader only makes cold starts faster, and provisioned concurrency keeps one copy warm; neither fills an idle GPU.

Model pinning keeps N warm copies of a component. What does that guard?

Pinning is the latency guard: N warm copies mean a demand surge scales out to copies that are already loaded, so nobody waits through a cold start. The other two answers are about cost and restart speed, not about answering on time.

You swap a model version with a blue-green update. What does it protect?

Blue-green builds the new version fully, flips traffic to it, and rolls back on failure — uptime is what it protects, at the price of briefly keeping two versions alive. Utilization and throughput are side effects at best.

Tiered KV cache plus intelligent routing: at which context does P50 TTFT fall the most?

Both numbers are real: 72% at 8k context, 94% at 64k. The longer the context, the heavier the key-value (KV) cache, so the bigger the win — that's the whole point of caching across tiers.
